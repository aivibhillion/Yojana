Yojana Chapter 10: Outcome-Based Budgeting in Health: Linking Public Financing with Measurable Results

India's health spending has grown; the challenge is ensuring money translates into quality services — Meghalaya's OBB pilot offers a live test.

Yojana · Current Affairs Notes · September 2026

Key Points at a Glance

  • GHE (National Health Accounts 2022-23): government health expenditure 1.15% of GDP (2013-14) → 1.43% (2022-23); per capita Rs 1,042 → Rs 2,786 (~2.7×); OOPE share 64.2% → 43.4%; still below the NHP 2017 target of 2.5%.
  • OBB links funds to assessed performance against defined indicators; unlike the Outcome Budget (a disclosure document), performance determines fund release and fixes accountability on the implementing entity.
  • Meghalaya: OBB Implementation Framework notified 16 December 2025; pilot Oct 2025–Mar 2026 across ~89 entities; statewide expansion planned from FY 2026-27; predecessor IPA under World Bank-supported MHSSP (Oct 2023).
  • Scorecards: up to 1,000 points; funds earned = allocation × external performance score; max annual allocations: MCH hospital Rs 70 lakh, CHC Rs 35 lakh, PHC Rs 21 lakh, UPHC Rs 10 lakh, state dispensary Rs 15 lakh.
  • Caution: evidence of effect is limited (pre-post, no counterfactual); risks include gaming, neglect of unmeasured care, penalising disadvantaged facilities and verification costs.

Health financing trends

  • GHE rose from 1.15% of GDP (2013-14) to 1.43% (2022-23) (1.48% on the revised GDP series); per capita government spending rose ~2.7× (Rs 1,042 → Rs 2,786).
  • Government's share of total health expenditure rose 28.6% → 43.7%; out-of-pocket expenditure fell 64.2% → 43.4%.
  • GHE peaked at 1.84% of GDP in 2021-22 (COVID-19 response); still below the National Health Policy 2017 target of 2.5% of GDP.
  • Major programmes: NHM, Ayushman Bharat, AB-PMJAY expanded access and financial protection; the challenge is converting investment into quality at the point of care.

Understanding OBB and related concepts

  • OBB = funds allocated to spending entities are linked to assessed performance against defined indicators; accountability rests with the implementing entity.
  • Outcome Budget (India) is a disclosure document tabled with the Budget that maps outlays to outputs/outcomes — it does not make allocation contingent on achievement.
  • RBF (results-based financing) releases funds only against verified results, is usually project-linked and frequently donor- or project-financed; IPA under MHSSP is an RBF mechanism, whereas OBB works through government budget heads.
  • Performance-based budgeting (PBB) is the umbrella family of procedures linking funds to performance information.
  • India's building blocks: Union Outcome Budget and Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework; NHM SPIPs/ROPs and a performance-linked share of flexipool (20% in 2018-19); AB-PMJAY strategic purchasing.
  • Source note: the article's text and table give different start years for the Outcome Budget / Output-Outcome Monitoring Framework (text: 2017-18; table: Outcome Budget since 2005-06, OOMF later). The Union Outcome Budget dates from 2005-06; verify exact years before quoting.

Meghalaya's experience

  • IPA (Internal Performance Agreements) under the World Bank-supported MHSSP: RBF for selected entities from October 2023.
  • OBB Implementation Framework notified 16 December 2025; pilot Oct 2025–Mar 2026 across ~89 entities not receiving IPA funds; statewide from FY 2026-27.
  • Scorecards up to 1,000 points (quality management, administration, staff competency, community engagement, drugs, labs, insurance utilisation, services); funds = allocation × external score.
  • Max annual allocations: MCH hospital Rs 70 lakh; CHC Rs 35 lakh; PHC Rs 21 lakh; UPHC Rs 10 lakh; state dispensary Rs 15 lakh — additional to NHM, insurance and other scheme funds.
  • Verification: self-assessment, then external assessment by an authority one level higher (District Quality Assessment Teams; Joint Directorate Assessment Teams); JDQC is the nodal authority; NQAS-trained assessors expected.
  • Funds sit in a separate OBB bank account with dual signatories; release needs an approved action plan and (after the first cycle) a utilisation certificate; start-up grants ease capacity constraints.

Implementation considerations and risks

  • Evidence of OBB's effect is limited: predecessor IPA shows score improvements but only pre-post comparisons without a counterfactual.
  • Equity: remote PHCs with vacancies and weak supply may score lower despite greater need — suggests baseline-based scoring or weights for remoteness and disadvantage.
  • Risks: neglecting unmeasured care, avoiding difficult cases, overstating performance/gaming scores, penalising disadvantaged facilities, verification costs exceeding incentives.
  • Fiscal safeguard: OBB funds are additional to existing financing, protecting continuity of essential services; scaling should protect core funding and link only a calibrated share to performance.
  • Recommended safeguards: assessor rotation, sample re-verification, transparent appeals, periodic independent evaluation, equity adjustments in indicators, reliable data systems (HMIS, NQAS), and budgeting for recurring assessment costs.

From spending more to spending better

India's public health spending has risen (1.15% → 1.43% of GDP; per capita 2.7×; OOPE share down to 43.4%), yet remains below the NHP 2017 goal of 2.5% — making efficiency of utilisation the central question. The article's thesis: public finance should focus not only on how much is spent but on what it delivers.

A funding gap persists in facility readiness and service quality, which existing channels (NHM, insurance, state budgets, untied funds) finance only indirectly. OBB is intended to close this gap.

Terminology that exam answers must keep distinct

  • Performance-based budgeting: umbrella concept
  • Outcome Budget: disclosure/accountability document; allocation not contingent on results
  • OBB: government budget-execution rule — funds released against assessed performance
  • RBF: pays on verified results; typically project-linked and often donor-financed

Meghalaya's IPA (RBF, MHSSP) and OBB (budget heads, ongoing) show how the two differ in practice.

Meghalaya as a policy experiment

Strengths: clear allocation formula, level-specific scorecards, defined assessment roles, ring-fenced accounts, dual signatories, start-up funding. Weaknesses: evidence of effect is limited; hierarchical (one-level-up) assessment is not independent third-party verification and can create collegial pressure and inconsistent interpretation.

The article therefore treats it as an evolving policy experiment needing careful monitoring, independent evaluation and transparent reporting.

Way forward

  • Scale gradually: protect core funding; link a calibrated share to performance
  • Indicators on recipient-controlled factors with equity adjustments
  • Credible, contestable, affordable verification (sample-based external checks)
  • For other States: complement, not replace, NHM/AB-PMJAY financing; match design to health-system capacity
