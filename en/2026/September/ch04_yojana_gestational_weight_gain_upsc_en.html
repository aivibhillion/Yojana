Yojana Chapter 4: Beyond the Weighing Scale: Strengthening Gestational Weight Gain

Gestational weight gain (GWG) is a modifiable predictor of birth and long-term health outcomes — but India still lacks its own GWG standards.

Yojana · Current Affairs Notes · September 2026

Key Points at a Glance

  • IOM 2009 BMI-specific GWG ranges: underweight 12.5–18 kg; normal 11.5–16 kg; overweight 7–11.5 kg; obesity 5–9 kg. FOGSI follows IOM.
  • India: no nationally derived GWG standards; MoHFW advises ~9–11 kg average gain; POSHAN Abhiyaan guidelines say at least 10–12 kg (≥ ~1 kg/month in the last six months). GWG is a POSHAN Tracker indicator since 2024.
  • WHO began work in 2023–25 on global GWG standards (still underway); ICMR is pooling Indian cohort data for context-specific recommendations.
  • Excess GWG → gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, macrosomia, LGA, caesarean; inadequate GWG → low birth weight (LBW), SGA, preterm birth.
  • Rajasthan cash-plus + SBCC: across 90,732 births, mean birth weight +71.3 g and LBW down 7.4 percentage points.

Standards and guidance

  • GWG is the total weight gained from conception until labour/delivery.
  • The 2009 IOM recommendations (also referenced by WHO antenatal guidance) give BMI-specific ranges.
  • GWG is monitored in the POSHAN Tracker since 2024; routine weight measurement at ANC is recommended.
  • Most GWG charts come from high-income countries; IOM applicability to Asian populations (different BMI cut-offs) is uncertain.
  • MoHFW: average ~9–11 kg; MWCD/POSHAN Abhiyaan: at least 10–12 kg, minimum ~1 kg/month in the last six months.
  • FOGSI recommendations follow IOM BMI-specific ranges; ICMR is building an Indian evidence base through pooled cohort data to inform context-specific recommendations.

Evidence from Asia and India

  • A systematic review of over 1 million pregnant women: 47% gained more than IOM-recommended, 23% gained less.
  • WINGS (North India): 40.2% of women had GWG below IOM; each extra 100 g/week of GWG ↔ +61 g birth weight and +0.16 cm birth length.
  • Gujarat study: 92.75% of women who gained less than recommended had newborns with significant LBW.
  • Mother and Infant (MAI) cohort, rural Maharashtra: inadequate GWG linked with higher LBW and SGA; associations vary by pre-pregnancy BMI, so BMI-specific recommendations are needed.

Interventions that worked

  • Rajasthan cash-plus: direct benefit transfer + comprehensive SBCC (home counselling, group sessions, mass and digital media).
  • Rajasthan results (vs 2022): cash used for food 30% → 89% (2025); local foods +49%; myths and taboos −35%; men's awareness 18% → 62%.
  • Evaluation: 60,899 women — third-trimester weight +3.4 kg; 90,732 births — birth weight +71.3 g, LBW −7.4 percentage points.
  • Haryana behavioural trial: supervised exercise cut postpartum weight retention most (3.25 kg), then pedometer + text messages (2.81 kg).
  • Alive & Thrive (Uttar Pradesh): intensified ANC (micronutrients, interpersonal counselling, community mobilisation) gave modest gains; the WINGS trial added pre-conception care, WASH and extra food support when GWG fell below IOM.

Why GWG deserves policy attention

GWG is both a marker of pregnancy progression and a potentially modifiable predictor of birth and long-term outcomes for mother and child. Excess gain raises risks of gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders, macrosomia, LGA births, caesarean delivery and postpartum weight retention; inadequate gain is linked with LBW, SGA and preterm birth.

In a country with persistent LBW and maternal undernutrition, GWG is a cheap, routinely measurable early-warning indicator — which is why its inclusion in the POSHAN Tracker (2024) matters.

The standards problem

India has no nationally derived, population-specific GWG standards; FOGSI follows IOM, MoHFW and MWCD give slightly different ranges, and global charts are built on high-income populations. WHO's global standard work (2023–25) is still underway and ICMR is pooling Indian cohorts.

Associations vary by pre-pregnancy BMI — hence the need for BMI-specific recommendations and monitoring.

What the intervention evidence shows

Evidence supports integrated approaches — nutrition counselling, physical activity, regular monitoring, supplementation, community support and cash-plus transfers. The Rajasthan cash-plus + SBCC programme shifted both household behaviour (cash used for food, men's awareness) and outcomes (birth weight, LBW).

Caveat: pre-post and programme evaluations show association, not clean causal effects; Haryana's trial did not change total GWG significantly but helped women stay within the recommended range.

Way forward

  • Move from routine weighing to regular monitoring, counselling and timely action at ANC
  • Start nutrition and lifestyle counselling early; target women at risk of inadequate/excess gain
  • Pair PMMVY and POSHAN 2.0 with GWG and birth-weight tracking as linked outcomes
  • Build context-specific standards, better data capture and SBCC; train frontline workers and provide weighing equipment
