Yojana Chapter 5: Gut Health and Nutrition: The Science of the Human Microbiome

The microbiome has reshaped our understanding of physiology — but clinical translation must stay disciplined. The strongest prescription today is a varied, minimally processed diet.

Yojana · Current Affairs Notes · September 2026

Key Points at a Glance

  • Microbiome = community of bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses plus their genes and metabolic activity; the largest reservoir is the colon; ~40 trillion bacterial cells in a reference adult.
  • Fibre fermentation yields short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) — acetate, propionate, butyrate; butyrate fuels colonocytes and supports barrier integrity and immune regulation.
  • There is no universal healthy microbiome; ‘good/bad’ labels are contextual, and consumer microbiome reports are unreliable for clinical use.
  • Food-first: aim for at least 25 g naturally occurring fibre/day (WHO-consistent), raised gradually with hydration; weekly diversity beats chasing one ‘superfood’.
  • Faecal microbiota therapy: clearest evidence-based role is preventing recurrent *C. difficile* infection after standard antibiotics; for ulcerative colitis/Crohn's it remains in clinical trials.

The gut as an ecosystem

  • The gut is a co-evolved ecosystem, not a passive digestive tube; microbes are mostly anaerobic.
  • Functions: break down indigestible substrates, synthesise selected vitamins, modify bile acids and drugs, resist pathogens, educate the immune system.
  • Altered gut communities are reported in IBS, IBD, colorectal cancer, coeliac disease, obesity and diabetes — but causality is often uncertain.
  • Dysbiosis helps understand pathology but is not necessarily a medical target; disease, medication, diet restriction and transit can themselves reshape the microbiome.
  • Gut–brain, gut–liver and gut–metabolic axes are of interest, but we are not yet at the point of using the gut microbiome to influence other organs. Most data come from faecal samples, though the microbiome varies by gut segment; SIBO (upper-gut bacterial overgrowth) can cause inflammation irrespective of ‘good’ or ‘bad’ bacteria.

Indian and South Asian context

  • Plant-based, higher-fibre cohorts commonly show Prevotella-enriched communities; greater animal-protein and fat intake shows Bacteroides-associated patterns.
  • In many Indian tribal populations *Prevotella copri* exceeds 25% of gut bacteria; *Bifidobacterium* is more abundant where dairy intake is high.
  • Migration cohorts show dietary acculturation can shift the microbiome towards a western profile; ultra-processed foods add adverse microbial exposure to cardiometabolic risk.

Food-first approach and therapies

  • Increase dietary diversity with minimally processed foods: whole grains and millets, pulses, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds.
  • Target: at least 25 g fibre/day for adults and older adolescents (WHO-consistent), increased gradually with adequate hydration.
  • Traditional fermented foods (curd with live cultures, kanji, fermented batters) may contribute microbial compounds; a small feeding study showed more diversity and lower inflammatory markers — not a disease-treatment trial.
  • Probiotics and prebiotics are wellness strategies; impact in symptomatic disease is limited and they are not a frontline clinical option.
  • Faecal microbiota therapy is a medical intervention, not a wellness procedure; it carries a risk of transmitting infectious organisms.
  • Fermented foods or high-fibre intake can aggravate symptoms in some patients — tolerance, food safety and the overall dietary pattern remain central; the author's bottom line is a varied, culturally appropriate, minimally processed diet, regular physical activity, adequate sleep and prudent medication use.

What the science supports — and what it does not

The microbiome expands human metabolic and immune capacity (SCFA production, bile-acid and drug modification, colonisation resistance). Yet clinical translation must stay disciplined: a healthy gut cannot yet be reduced to a diversity score, one bacterium or a capsule.

Because there is no universal healthy microbiome, ‘good’ and ‘bad’ bacterial labels are contextual and potentially misleading; consumer microbiome reports are unreliable for clinical use.

Causality, context and the Indian lens

Associations between altered microbiomes and diseases (IBS, IBD, colorectal cancer, obesity, diabetes) are plentiful, but the direction of causality is unclear.

India is a valuable research setting because diet, geography, sanitation, medication exposure, urbanisation and socioeconomic conditions are highly heterogeneous. Diet-linked patterns (Prevotella vs Bacteroides; Bifidobacterium with dairy) are consistent findings, but health labels remain misleading.

Therapies in perspective

Probiotics/prebiotics: wellness use, limited impact in symptomatic disease. FMT: evidence-based for recurrent *C. difficile* after standard antibiotics in appropriately selected patients; other indications (UC, Crohn's) belong in trials; infection-transmission risk demands caution.

Precision microbiome care is a compelling future direction but needs further scientific exploration.

Practical takeaway

  • Diverse, culturally appropriate, minimally processed diet (millets, pulses, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds)
  • ≥25 g fibre/day, raised gradually with hydration
  • Regular physical activity, adequate sleep, prudent use of medicines (especially antibiotics)
  • Treat traditional fermented foods as helpful complements, not treatments
