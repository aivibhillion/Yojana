Yojana Chapter 8: Rabies: A Fatal, Yet Vaccine-Preventable Disease

Almost universally fatal once symptoms appear, rabies is preventable through prompt wound care, vaccines and immunoglobulins.

Yojana · Current Affairs Notes · September 2026

Key Points at a Glance

  • In India ~97% of human rabies is transmitted by dogs, ~2% by cats and ~1% by wild animals; over 90% of dog-transmitted rabies comes from stray dogs (~1.5 crore stray dogs, roughly 1 per 100 persons).
  • First aid: wash all bite wounds with soap and running water for up to 15 minutes, then apply antiseptic; do not apply turmeric, chilli, etc.; do not suture immediately.
  • PEP vaccine schedules: intradermal (0.1 mL, two sites) days 0-3-7-28; intramuscular days 0-3-7-14-28. Intradermal route approved by WHO and in practice in India since February 2006.
  • Category III exposures (bleeding wounds) need RIG or RmAb as well as vaccine. RmAbs (single mAb 2017; cocktail 2020) are in post-marketing surveillance, awaiting inclusion in national guidelines.
  • NAPRE (September 2021): goal of zero human deaths from dog-mediated rabies by 2030; delivered through State Action Plans (SAPRE). India accounts for about one-third of the global burden.

Disease basics

  • Caused by the rabies virus present in saliva of rabid animals; no cure once symptoms appear; the virus travels along nerves to the brain (encephalitis) causing respiratory failure or cardiac arrest.
  • Most common mode: bite of a rabid animal; rarely through scratches (esp. cats) or licks on an existing wound.
  • Incubation: usually 3–6 weeks; as short as 4 days or over a year; faster for bites on the face, head, neck, hands and fingers.
  • Two forms: furious (~80%) — hydrophobia, aerophobia, photophobia, death in 3–7 days; paralytic (~20%) — gradual paralysis over 2–3 weeks, often after incomplete prophylaxis.
  • Drinking milk from a suspect rabid animal is not known to transmit rabies; boiling milk destroys the virus. Bats are not known to transmit rabies in India; ordinary house rats do not transmit it.
  • Since 2022, most States/UTs have made human rabies a notifiable disease under State infectious-disease acts, so all facilities, including private hospitals, must report cases.

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)

  • PEP has three steps: physical removal (water), chemical neutralisation (soap, antiseptic), biological neutralisation (vaccine/immunoglobulin).
  • Wash with soap and running water for up to 15 minutes; do not apply turmeric, red chilli, tea/coffee powder or plant juices; do not suture immediately.
  • Modern vaccines (purified Vero cell, purified chick embryo cell) are given in the shoulder in adults and anterolateral thigh in infants/toddlers.
  • Schedules: intradermal (0.1 mL at two sites) days 0-3-7-28; intramuscular (full vial) days 0-3-7-14-28. Day 0 is the day of the first dose, not necessarily the day of the bite.
  • RIG is for category III wounds (oozing blood/frank bleeding) because vaccine alone takes ~10–14 days to protect; HRIG (imported, no skin test) and ERIG (Indian, skin test needed) are the two types; infiltrated into and around all wounds.
  • RmAbs are recombinant, free from horse/human serum, need no skin test, priced between ERIG and HRIG; single mAb (2017) and two-mAb cocktail (2020), produced in India.
  • Counselling matters: bite victims are often distressed; there are no dietary restrictions during PEP; pregnancy and lactation are not contraindications; immunosuppressant use needs modified PEP.

Pre-exposure prophylaxis and dog management

  • PrEP: three doses on days 0, 7, and 21 or 28 (IM full vial or ID 0.1 mL at one site); later exposure needs only two booster doses and no RIG/RmAb.
  • At-risk groups: veterinarians, pet owners, zoo and forest staff, police/military, municipal dog catchers, delivery persons, laboratory and vaccine-manufacturing staff, travellers.
  • ABC programme (launched 2001, amended 2023): catch, vaccinate, neuter/sterilise stray dogs and release them in the same area.
  • 2025 Supreme Court suo motu case (Delhi): hospitals, educational institutions, bus stands, railway stations and similar public places to be made ‘stray dog free’ and dogs moved to shelters.
  • NAPRE launched September 2021 (target: zero dog-mediated human rabies deaths by 2030); health being a State subject, delivery is through SAPRE.
  • Global: ~59,000 human deaths a year; India accounted for ~20,000 in 2003 (about one-third). The 2015 call for elimination by 2030 came jointly from WHO, WOAH, FAO and GARC. The author recommends a 100% centrally sponsored mission-mode programme with mass dog vaccination, free complete PEP, active surveillance and awareness.

Why rabies is a governance problem, not only a medical one

Rabies is almost universally fatal yet 100% vaccine-preventable. India accounts for about one-third of the global burden, so elimination by 2030 (NAPRE) is a stretch target that depends on dog vaccination, free and complete PEP, surveillance and awareness — not on clinical advances.

Because health is a State subject, NAPRE relies on SAPREs; notifiability since 2022 in most States improves surveillance.

Myths and practice gaps

The article stresses first aid (15-minute washing), warns against turmeric/chilli/branding, and corrects misconceptions: the intradermal route is WHO-approved and equally effective to intramuscular — not inferior; milk from a rabid animal is not a transmission route; PEP is safe in pregnancy.

Children often hide bites for fear of reprisal, so parents must examine them for even minor scratches.

The stray-dog dilemma

With ~1.5 crore stray dogs and >90% of dog-transmitted rabies from strays, the issue is contentious: the ABC programme (2001; amended 2023) releases sterilised, vaccinated dogs back, often against residents' wishes; the 2025 Supreme Court suo motu ruling pushed for ‘stray dog free’ safe zones in public institutions. The article calls this an evolving stage.

Compulsory licensing and annual vaccination of pet dogs and responsible pet ownership are part of the recommended mix.

Way forward

  • Mass vaccination of dogs (pets and strays) as the core elimination lever
  • Free, complete PEP for all bite victims; RmAbs after inclusion in national guidelines
  • Active surveillance, mass awareness (esp. school children)
  • Mission-mode, 100% centrally sponsored programme (author's recommendation); strong SAPREs
