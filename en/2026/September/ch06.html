Yojana Chapter 6: Towards a Healthier India: Combating Lifestyle Diseases through Nutrition, Activity and Policy

Lifestyle diseases are not simply individual choices — food environments, urbanisation and gaps in preventive care shape them.

Yojana · Current Affairs Notes · September 2026

Key Points at a Glance

  • Lifestyle diseases = NCDs such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, obesity and metabolic syndrome, driven by diets, inactivity, nutrition challenges and prevention gaps.
  • NPCDCS (2010) expanded into NP-NCD: health promotion, population-based screening of people aged 30+, early diagnosis, treatment, follow-up and referral.
  • Ayushman Arogya Mandir (AAM) (formerly Health and Wellness Centres): promotes healthy diets, yoga, exercise, tobacco cessation, self-care plus screening, medicines, diagnostics and referral.
  • Nutrition side: Poshan Abhiyaan (2018), Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0, and FSSAI's Eat Right India — Eat Safe, Eat Healthy, Eat Sustainable.
  • Way forward: coordinate (don't silo) NCD, nutrition and activity efforts — affordable diverse foods, everyday physical activity, early screening, early nutrition education, community-based promotion.

Drivers of lifestyle diseases

  • India is in a health transition: infectious disease control and life expectancy have improved while the NCD burden is growing.
  • Changing diets: more highly processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages alongside traditional diets rich in whole grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits and nuts.
  • Double burden: undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies coexist with overweight and diet-related NCDs; food safety (unsafe food and water) also undermines nutrition.
  • Physical inactivity: urban living, sedentary work and digital devices; activity is not only gym workouts — safe public spaces and walking/cycling infrastructure matter.
  • Lifestyle diseases develop silently for years; prevention cannot rest on hospitals alone — schools, workplaces, communities, food systems and urban environments all shape health, making this a broader public-health challenge, not merely a medical one.

Existing programmes

  • NPCDCS (launched 2010) → NP-NCD: national framework for NCD prevention and management; screening for people aged 30 years and above.
  • Poshan Abhiyaan (launched 2018) targets children, adolescents, pregnant women and lactating mothers; Mission Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0 deliver supplementary nutrition, nutrition/health education, check-ups and referrals via Anganwadis.
  • FSSAI is an autonomous statutory body under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare; its Eat Right India movement has three themes: Eat Safe, Eat Healthy, Eat Sustainable.
  • National Health Mission works towards universal, equitable, affordable healthcare and aligns with SDG 3 (good health and well-being).
  • The national NCD portal supports enrolment and management of people with hypertension and diabetes — digital systems should complement, not replace frontline care.

Future recommendations

  • Make healthier diets easier: affordable access to diverse nutritious foods — whole grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits, appropriate protein.
  • Build physical activity into everyday environments (schools, workplaces, communities, safe walking/cycling).
  • Strengthen early screening and primary healthcare: reliable medicines, diagnostics, trained health workers, referral systems.
  • Start nutrition education early, involving families, schools and communities; address undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies and overweight together.
  • Adopt a coordinated community-based approach — government agencies, health workers, schools, workplaces, local organisations and food businesses; health messages should be culturally appropriate, affordable and realistic.

From personal choice to environment and policy

The article reframes NCDs: they are not simply the result of individual choices. Rapid urbanisation, sedentary occupations and changing food environments make prevention harder, so policy must make healthy choices accessible and practical.

India faces a double burden: undernutrition and micronutrient deficiency coexist with overweight and diet-related NCDs; food safety adds another dimension.

Architecture of the response

The response is layered: NP-NCD (screening, treatment, referral), AAM (primary-care promotion and screening), Poshan Abhiyaan/Poshan 2.0 (life-course nutrition), FSSAI's Eat Right India (food-environment and consumer-facing), and NHM (health-system backbone aligned with SDG 3).

The article stresses that digital tools (NCD portal) must complement — not replace — trained workers, reliable medicines and diagnostics, and functioning referral networks.

Critical appraisal

Strength: a comprehensive multi-programme architecture with a strong preventive and primary-care orientation. Gap highlighted: the programmes address different pieces and need coordination rather than parallel running; early detection depends on reliable supplies and referral.

A balanced answer should also note that physical activity is a built-environment and policy question (safe public spaces, walking/cycling infrastructure), not just exercise advice.

Way forward

  • Affordable access to diverse, nutritious food; reduced exposure to processed products
  • Everyday physical activity in schools, workplaces and communities
  • Early screening plus reliable primary care and referral
  • Early nutrition education involving families and schools; culturally appropriate health messaging
